Meal Prep for Beginners: A Simple 5-Step Start Guide

You don't need fancy equipment, hours of free time, or culinary experience. You need two recipes, five containers, and about 90 minutes. Here's how to do your first meal prep session — without the overwhelm.

Why Beginners Usually Quit After One Week

Most first-time meal preppers make the same mistake: they try to do too much, too soon. They research 10 different recipes, spend four hours in the kitchen on Sunday, make something they're already bored of by Tuesday, and then don't do it again.

The problem isn't meal prep. It's starting too big. The goal for your first session isn't to build the perfect system — it's to finish the session, actually eat the food, and want to do it again the following week. That's it. Everything else is secondary.

"Your first prep session should take 90 minutes and produce five lunches. Nothing more ambitious than that."

What You Actually Need to Get Started

Before you cook anything, you need a few basics. This is a deliberately short list — don't let anyone tell you that you need a spiraliser or an Instant Pot to start meal prepping.

The starter kit

  • 5 matching meal prep containers — glass or BPA-free plastic, all the same size so they stack. Budget about £15–25 for a set of 10.
  • A kitchen scale — weighing ingredients (especially protein) makes hitting your targets predictable. A basic digital scale costs about £8.
  • A baking tray and one large saucepan — you almost certainly already have these.
  • Two recipes — just two. Not four, not six. Two things you actually want to eat five times.

That's genuinely all you need. The fancier equipment comes later if and when you want it.

Your First Meal Prep: The 5-Step System

1

Choose one protein and one carb source

Your first session should be a simple combination: one protein that's easy to cook in bulk, and one carb source. Good beginner options for protein: chicken breast (bake a tray at 200°C for 22–25 minutes), salmon fillets (same oven, 18–20 minutes), or lean beef mince (brown in a pan with seasoning). For carbs: rice (cook once, portion into containers), pasta, or roasted sweet potato. Pick things you already know how to cook. The goal right now isn't to try new things — it's to build the habit.

2

Decide how many portions you need

For your first week, aim for five lunches. That's five containers, one per weekday. If you also want to cover dinners, double the quantities. Don't overthink the maths — if you're cooking five lunches and you want roughly 40g of protein per meal, you need 200g of cooked protein in total. For chicken breast, that's about 250–280g raw (it loses weight during cooking). For rice, a standard 50g dry serving per container works well. Write this down before you shop.

3

Shop the day before (or the morning of)

Write your shopping list based on step 2. Keep it short: your protein, your carb, two or three vegetables to roast, and any sauce or seasoning you want. A beginners' shop for five lunches costs around £12–20. Buy on Saturday so everything is fresh on Sunday. Avoid the trap of buying too many ingredients "just in case" — unused fresh food just creates waste and guilt.

4

Cook in the right order (longest first)

When you're ready to cook, start with whatever takes the longest. If chicken is going in the oven, get it in first. While it's cooking, cook the rice on the hob, chop and season the vegetables, and prep any sauce. By the time the oven timer goes, your rice is done and your vegetables are in. Everything finishes within 15 minutes of each other. Total active cooking time for a beginner session: around 30–40 minutes. Total elapsed time: 60–90 minutes including cleanup.

5

Portion, seal, label, store

While everything is still slightly warm (not hot — let it cool for 10 minutes first), portion it into your containers. Use the scale: measure your protein per container, add your carbs, add the veg, seal the lid. Write the date on the container with masking tape or a label. Days 1–4 go in the fridge. If you've prepped day 5 as well, put that container in the freezer and move it to the fridge the night before. That's your system. It takes about 15 minutes to portion five meals.

The 3 Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Picking recipes that don't hold

Some foods don't survive 4 days in the fridge. Dressed salad leaves wilt and turn unpleasant by day two. Avocado browns. Anything with a crispy coating goes soft. Stick to foods that actually keep: cooked chicken, rice, roasted vegetables, grains, soups, stews. These all taste perfectly fine (often better, because flavours develop) after a few days in the fridge.

Mistake 2: Not portioning before storing

If you put a big pot of rice in the fridge and plan to "scoop some out each day", you'll either overeat it, undereat it, or — most likely — eat more of something else because scooping rice into a container at 7am is annoying. Portion everything into individual containers immediately after cooking. This single habit is what separates meal preppers who actually hit their targets from those who don't.

Mistake 3: Making food you don't really like

There's no virtue in eating plain boiled chicken and unseasoned broccoli for a week. If you don't like it, you won't eat it — and then you'll order a pizza on Wednesday and feel like you failed. Season your food generously. Use sauces you enjoy. Make things that taste good. The best meal prep is the food you'd actually choose to eat.

Good Beginner Recipes to Start With

Here are five meal combinations that are genuinely beginner-friendly, taste good on day 4, and are easy to scale:

  • Teriyaki chicken + jasmine rice + edamame — season chicken thighs with store-bought teriyaki sauce, bake at 200°C for 28 minutes, slice over rice with frozen edamame (just microwave them).
  • Baked salmon + sweet potato + green beans — lemon, olive oil, garlic on the salmon. Everything on one tray. 20 minutes at 200°C.
  • Lean beef mince bolognese + pasta — brown mince with onion and garlic, add a jar of good tomato sauce, simmer 15 minutes. Cook pasta separately, portion together. Keeps 4–5 days.
  • Chicken and rice bowl with roasted peppers and onion — the classic. Season chicken, roast vegetables alongside it, serve over rice. Season aggressively and it's genuinely good.
  • Greek-style baked chicken + bulgur wheat + cucumber and tomato — oregano, lemon, olive oil on the chicken. Bulgur cooks in 15 minutes. Add cucumber and tomato fresh each day.

How Long Does a Beginner Meal Prep Take?

Your first session will probably take about 90 minutes start to finish including cleanup. After a month of weekly sessions, you'll be doing it in 60 minutes. After three months, it'll feel like a half-hour task. The slowest part is the first few sessions, where you're figuring out timing and quantities. Once you find your rhythm, it gets significantly faster.

The Sunday evening payoff: zero daily cooking for five lunches. If you spend 90 minutes Sunday, you save at least 10–15 minutes every weekday morning or lunchtime — that's an hour saved back over the week, plus money saved, plus better food. The maths always works in favour of prepping.

Frequently Asked Questions

I don't have much time. Is meal prep actually worth it?
90 minutes on Sunday saves you at least 10–15 minutes every weekday (not cooking, not deciding, not queuing). That's 50–75 minutes saved over the week. It's almost always worth it on pure time grounds, before you factor in money and eating better.
Do I need to count calories to meal prep?
No — but meal prep and calorie tracking pair well because you cook the same things in the same quantities, so you only need to log a recipe once. If you're not tracking at all, meal prep still helps you eat more consistently and spend less. You don't need a macro target to benefit from having food ready in the fridge.
What's the best day to meal prep?
Sunday is most common because it sets up the working week. But it's whatever day you have 90 minutes free and you can shop the day before. Some people do Saturday. Some do a smaller session Sunday and another midweek. Pick what fits your schedule — there's no rule that it has to be Sunday.
Can I meal prep if I live alone?
Meal prep is particularly well-suited to living alone. You cook for yourself, portion for yourself, and don't have to negotiate what to eat with anyone else. The portions are consistent, waste is low (you use exactly what you need), and nothing sits in the fridge going off because someone else ate it first.
What if I get bored eating the same thing?
Most people who meal prep find the boredom fades after a few weeks — partly because you get used to it, and partly because you start building a rotation of 6–8 recipes you actually enjoy. You're not eating the same thing every week forever; you're picking two options per week from a growing list of meals you like. And if you really can't face day 4 of the same meal, swap one of your containers for something different. Meal prep is a framework, not a prison.
