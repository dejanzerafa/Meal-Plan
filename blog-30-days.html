I Ate the Same 2 Meals Every Day for 30 Days — Here's Exactly What Happened

Not a controlled study. Not a sponsored post. Just me, two containers, and a month of eating the exact same food every single day — to see whether the consistency actually did anything, or whether it would just make me miserable.

Why I Did This

I'd been "eating well" for about six months. Or so I told myself. I tracked macros intermittently, swapped between dozens of different recipes, spent Sunday afternoons browsing meal prep YouTube, and still didn't really know whether my diet was doing what I wanted it to do. My body composition hadn't moved meaningfully in eight weeks, and I kept blaming training when, honestly, I had no idea what I was actually eating.

The problem wasn't knowledge. I knew protein targets. I knew calorie ranges. The problem was decision fatigue. Every day involved a dozen small food choices — what's for lunch today, is this restaurant option going to wreck my macros, should I have a snack — and those decisions accumulated into a kind of nutritional chaos where nothing was actually being tracked properly.

So I decided to remove the decisions entirely. Two meals, every day, for 30 days. No exceptions on weekdays. Reasonable flexibility on weekends (I still ate out Saturday nights — I'm not a monk). The goal wasn't to prove anything dramatic. It was to give my diet a known baseline and see what happened when I actually controlled the variable.

The Meals

I kept this simple deliberately. Two meals I'd already made before, that I knew I could prep in under an hour for a full week, and that I actually liked eating.

Meal 1 — Lunch
Chicken, Rice & Broccoli
450 kcal 48g P 45g C 6g F
Meal 2 — Dinner
Salmon, Sweet Potato & Spinach
420 kcal 38g P 40g C 10g F

Two servings of each per day. Daily total: roughly 1,740 kcal and 172g of protein. (I know — that sounds like a lot of protein. It's on the higher end of the evidence-based range for someone my size training 4x per week, but I wanted to make muscle retention a non-issue during what was effectively a moderate deficit.)

Breakfast was a coffee and, most days, a Greek yogurt with berries. I tracked it but didn't obsess over it — the two main meals were the controlled variable.

Week 1: The Weirdness

Days 1–7

The first few days felt fine, almost easy. I'd just cooked a fresh batch and everything tasted good. By day four, I opened my fridge and noticed that the chicken had started to smell... not off, exactly, but very present. That particular combination of cooked rice and brassica that was pleasant on Monday had become aggressively familiar by Thursday. I ate it anyway.

The social awkwardness started on day five. A coworker asked me to grab lunch and I explained what I was doing. She looked at me the way you'd look at someone who'd just told you they were doing a juice cleanse, except with more pity. "What do you mean the same meal? Like, the exact same?" Yes. Exact same. I ate my container at my desk.

What surprised me in week one wasn't the food — it was how much mental space opened up. By day six I'd completely stopped thinking about what I was eating. The question "what should I have for lunch?" had just... disappeared. That was genuinely strange.

Week 2: The Groove

Days 8–14

Something shifted around day nine. I stopped noticing the monotony. The meals became background noise — something to eat, not something to think about. I ate lunch in about 12 minutes, which had never happened before in my life. Usually lunch is a 40-minute production of deciding, waiting, eating, regretting.

Gym performance held steady through week two, which I'd been half-expecting to drop. I was essentially in a modest deficit — probably 300–400 kcal below maintenance — but I hit a small deadlift PR on day 11. I don't fully know why. My guess: my pre-workout nutrition was suddenly predictable and consistent, which meant I was never training depleted and never training overfed. Just... consistent.

The salmon started tasting better in week two. I don't know if my palate adjusted or if I'd gotten better at seasoning it, but by day 12 I was actually looking forward to dinner in a way I hadn't expected. Simplified food does something to your appreciation of what's there.

Week 3: What Changed Physically

Days 15–21

Week three is when things got interesting on the scale. I'd lost about 1.2kg by the end of day 21, which sounds modest but felt more meaningful than the number suggests. I'd been bouncing between the same 2kg range for two months before this. The consistency had finally nailed the calorie target.

More noticeably than weight: I could see a change in how clothes were fitting. Belt one notch tighter. A shirt I'd bought that had always been a bit snug across the shoulders sat differently. I wasn't suddenly lean — this wasn't a transformation — but the direction was unambiguous for the first time in months.

Hunger became predictable, which sounds like a small thing but isn't. I knew I'd be hungry at 12:30 and again at 6:30. That was it. No random 3pm crashes, no wondering why I was ravenous on Tuesday when I'd been fine on Monday. The body had settled into a rhythm.

"By week three I realised that what I'd been calling 'eating well' for the past six months was actually just eating unpredictably. Consistency, it turns out, is the variable I'd been missing."

Week 4: The Mental Shift

Days 22–30

The most unexpected thing about month four wasn't physical. It was mental.

I started noticing how much cognitive energy food decisions normally consumed. Not just the act of choosing — but the background hum of tracking, estimating, second-guessing restaurant menus, feeling vaguely guilty about having something unplanned, recalculating. It's a low-level drain that you don't notice until it's gone.

With both main meals set, that bandwidth went elsewhere. I started sleeping better, partly because I wasn't lying in bed reviewing what I'd eaten. Work felt clearer in the mornings. I don't want to over-credit two meals for all of this, but the correlation was hard to ignore.

By day 28, a different coworker — the one who'd been most vocal about this being a terrible idea — asked if I'd lost weight. I said yes, about 1.5kg at that point. She asked what I'd been doing. I told her. She said "that sounds miserable." It hadn't been.

The Results — Honest Numbers, Honest Caveats

What actually changed over 30 days

  • Weight: down 1.6kg (weekday average, not a single weigh-in — accounts for daily fluctuation)
  • Waist: approximately 2.5cm reduction based on belt notch and a soft tape measure
  • Gym lifts: held or marginally improved across the main compound movements
  • Sleep quality: subjectively better from week two onward (no tracker to confirm this)
  • Weekly food cost: reduced by roughly 30% vs my previous varied approach
  • Sunday prep time: 55 minutes for the full week's meals

The caveats matter here. This is n=1. I'm one person with a specific starting point, training history, and lifestyle. If your starting point is different, your results will be different. The meals I chose happen to suit my macros — they might not suit yours. 30 days is also long enough to establish a trend but not long enough to draw firm conclusions about anything.

I'm not claiming this is the optimal way to eat. I'm reporting what happened when I tried it.

What I Learned

Consistency beats variety in the short term. When you're trying to move body composition in a specific direction, unpredictability is the enemy. You can't optimise what you can't measure, and you can't measure what changes every day.

Batch cooking isn't a life hack — it's a decision eliminator. The hour on Sunday wasn't time saved; it was decision debt paid in advance. Every day I didn't have to think about food was a day where that energy went elsewhere.

Food boredom is real but it plateaus. The first week's monotony did fade. By week three the meals felt neutral — not exciting, not unpleasant. More like filling your car with petrol than eating a meal. That emotional neutrality around food, I suspect, is actually useful if your goal is physique-related.

The fridge does start to smell. Day five is the critical threshold. A good container with a proper seal makes an enormous difference. I switched containers mid-experiment and it helped more than I expected.

Would I Do It Again?

Yes. But modified. I now rotate between 2–3 meals per week instead of 1. I keep the chicken and rice as a staple, swap the second meal between salmon, turkey bolognese, and a lean beef stir fry depending on what I batch-cooked on Sunday.

The outcome is almost identical in terms of macro consistency — I know exactly what I'm eating — but the variety is enough to make the meals feel like food again rather than nutrition delivery. That seems like the right balance for the long term.

"The takeaway isn't 'eat the same thing forever.' It's 'reduce food decisions and the macros take care of themselves.' You don't need to eat identically — you need to eat predictably."

The 30-day experiment broke something I'd been stuck on for months. Not because two meals is the answer, but because it proved that when the variable is controlled, the results follow. I've been running a 2–3 meal rotation for three months since. Body composition has continued to move slowly in the right direction. Weekly food cost is down. Sunday is the only time I think about meals.

If you've been "eating well" for six months without results, this is worth trying. Not forever. Just long enough to find out what your diet actually looks like when the chaos is removed.
