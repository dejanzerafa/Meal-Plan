High Protein Meal Prep: How to Hit 150g+ Every Day

Most people don't eat enough protein — not because they don't know they should, but because they never make it convenient. High-protein meal prep fixes that. Here's how to structure a full week so your protein target is already handled before Monday morning starts.

Why Protein Is the Non-Negotiable

Of the three macronutrients, protein is the one that most directly affects body composition. It supports muscle retention during fat loss, drives muscle growth during a bulk, keeps you fuller for longer, and has the highest thermic effect of any macronutrient (meaning your body burns more energy digesting it than it does with carbs or fat).

The research is fairly consistent: most people benefit from 1.6–2.2g of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day. For an 80kg person, that's 128–176g of protein daily. Getting there consistently — not just on gym days, not just when you feel motivated — is what actually produces results. And the most reliable way to get there consistently is to prep your protein in advance.

"Missing your protein target on three out of seven days isn't an 86% success rate. It's half the week's muscle-building signal gone missing."

The High-Protein Foods Worth Building Around

Not all protein sources are equally practical for meal prep. You want foods that are high in protein per gram, cook well in bulk, keep in the fridge for 4–5 days, and don't turn into a texture disaster by day three. Here's what actually works:

Food (100g cooked) Protein Prep-friendliness
Chicken breast 31g Excellent — bake in bulk, keeps 4–5 days
Turkey mince 29g Excellent — brown in bulk, very versatile
Lean beef mince (5% fat) 28g Excellent — freeze extra portions easily
Salmon fillet 25g Good — best consumed within 3 days
Tuna (tinned in water) 26g Excellent — no cooking, add fresh daily
Greek yogurt (0% fat) 10g per 100g Excellent — no prep, great for breakfast
Cottage cheese 11g per 100g Excellent — no prep, high volume, filling
Eggs (large) 6g per egg Good — hard boil in batches, keep 5 days
Edamame (shelled) 11g per 100g Good — frozen, microwave, add to any meal

How to Structure a High-Protein Day

The key is distributing protein across meals rather than trying to hit your target in one or two sittings. Your body can only effectively use around 30–50g of protein for muscle protein synthesis per meal (though the rest is still used, just for energy). Spreading it out across 3–4 meals is the practical approach most people follow.

Here's how a 160g protein day breaks down across four eating occasions:

  • Breakfast: 35–40g protein
  • Lunch: 40–45g protein
  • Dinner: 45–50g protein
  • Snack: 25–30g protein

That's a completely achievable spread if your meals are structured around a protein anchor. The problem most people have is that they think about protein as an afterthought rather than as the centrepiece of each meal.

A Full High-Protein Meal Prep Week (Sample Plan)

Below is a worked example for someone targeting 160g protein per day at approximately 2,200 calories. All main meals are prepped Sunday; snacks are grab-and-go (no prep needed).

~38g protein
MealOvernight oats: 80g oats + 200g Greek yogurt (0% fat) + 30g whey protein + berries38g
Lunch (prepped Sunday)
~45g protein
Meal150g cooked chicken breast + 200g rice + roasted broccoli and peppers + teriyaki sauce48g
Snack (no prep)
~25g protein
Option A200g cottage cheese + rice cakes22g
Option B2 hard-boiled eggs + 30g beef jerky26g
Option CGreek yogurt (200g) + protein bar30g
Dinner (prepped Sunday)
~50g protein
Meal170g cooked lean beef mince (bolognese-style) + 90g pasta + parmesan52g

Total: approximately 158–165g protein depending on snack choice. This is a realistic, achievable daily total for someone who's prepped their meals in advance. Without prep, hitting 160g on a busy weekday is significantly harder.

The Sunday High-Protein Prep Session

Here's how to cook all of the above in a single session:

Oven (200°C, fan): 25 minutes

Tray 1: 750g chicken breast (season with garlic, paprika, salt, olive oil). This gives you five 150g portions for five lunches. Tray 2 (if your oven has space): roasted broccoli and peppers — one large tray, lightly oiled and seasoned.

Hob: 20 minutes

While the oven runs: cook 500g dry rice in a large pot (makes approximately 1.5kg cooked rice — enough for 7–8 portions). In a separate pan, brown 850g lean beef mince with onion, garlic, and tomato sauce. Simmer 12 minutes. You now have five dinner portions of bolognese.

Overnight oats: 10 minutes

Assemble five jars or containers with the oat mixture. No cooking required. These live in the fridge and are grabbed each morning. Total active time: about 35–40 minutes. Elapsed time including oven wait: 60–75 minutes.

Getting to High Protein Without Feeling Like You're Force-Feeding Yourself

One of the most common complaints about high-protein eating is that it feels like a chore — eating a mountain of chicken breast every day, being uncomfortably full, or choking down a sixth egg when you're not hungry.

The fix is density, not volume. Some protein sources are very calorie-light relative to the protein they deliver. Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, and egg whites are all extremely high in protein per calorie. Eating 300g of Greek yogurt gives you about 30g protein for around 180 calories — roughly the same protein as a 100g chicken breast, but a completely different eating experience.

The practical approach: use lean cooked proteins (chicken, turkey mince, white fish) as your lunch and dinner anchors where you want substantial food, and use dairy-based protein (yogurt, cottage cheese) for breakfast and snacks where high volume isn't the goal. That way you're not feeling like you're grinding through food at every meal.

5 High-Protein Recipes Worth Prepping This Week

  • Garlic butter chicken breast bowls — bake chicken thighs (higher protein density than breast per calorie) in garlic butter, serve over rice with roasted courgette. 44g protein per serving.
  • Turkey mince stuffed peppers — brown turkey mince with tomato, spices, and cooked rice, stuff into halved peppers, bake 20 minutes. 38g protein. Keeps beautifully for 4 days.
  • Salmon and sweet potato trays — salmon fillets with lemon and dill, roasted sweet potato and green beans on the same tray. 36g protein. Best in the first 3 days.
  • High-protein egg muffins — whisk 12 eggs with spinach, turkey bacon, and cheese, pour into muffin tins, bake 18 minutes at 180°C. 2 muffins = 22g protein. A great breakfast or snack prep.
  • Beef and lentil stew — lean beef mince + red lentils + canned tomatoes + stock. One pot, 30 minutes. High protein from both the beef (28g/100g) and lentils (9g/100g). Freezes perfectly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need protein shakes to hit high protein targets?
No — 150–160g of protein is entirely achievable through whole food if your meals are structured well. That said, a single protein shake (25–30g protein) makes the last 20–30g of your daily target very easy to hit on days when food intake is lower than planned. Shakes aren't necessary, but they're practical as a fallback. Don't use them as your primary protein strategy.
Is too much protein bad for kidneys?
In healthy adults with no pre-existing kidney conditions, the evidence does not support the idea that high protein intake (up to 2.2g/kg bodyweight) is harmful to kidneys. This concern is largely based on studies in people with pre-existing kidney disease, where protein restriction is medically indicated. If you have a kidney condition, speak to a doctor before significantly increasing protein intake. For healthy adults, the evidence supports high protein diets as safe and beneficial.
What's the best high-protein breakfast to prep in advance?
Overnight oats with added protein powder and Greek yogurt is probably the most practical — it takes 5 minutes to assemble five jars, keeps perfectly for 5 days, and delivers 35–40g protein without cooking. Egg muffins are a close second: prep 12 at once, refrigerate, microwave in the morning. Both beat trying to cook a high-protein breakfast at 7am every weekday.
How do I know if I'm eating enough protein without tracking?
The most practical non-tracking approach is the palm method: at each main meal, your protein source should be roughly the size of your palm (about 120–150g of cooked meat or fish). Three meals with palm-sized protein portions plus a high-protein snack will put most people in the 130–160g range. It's imprecise, but it works as a floor. If you want to know exactly where you are, track for a week — you might be surprised how far off your intuition is.
Can I eat the same high-protein meals every day?
Yes, and many people who make great progress do exactly this. Eating the same structured meals removes decision fatigue, makes tracking nearly automatic, and keeps shopping simple. The key is choosing meals you don't find boring after 4 days. If you genuinely enjoy a chicken and rice bowl, it can run for months. If you're eating something you dislike because it's "optimal", you'll eventually stop.
